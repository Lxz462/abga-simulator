    Master Electrolytes Clinical Knowledge Base

    Na⁺

    Sodium

    135 – 145 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Body's Water Magnet & Osmotic Anchor: 90% of sodium stays outside cells. Where sodium goes, water follows. High or low sodium levels almost NEVER mean you ate too much or too little salt—they tell you whether your blood has too little or too much water!

    How the Body Controls It

    When blood gets 1% too concentrated, the brain triggers thirst and releases ADH (Vasopressin). ADH acts like a valve in the kidney collecting duct, saving free water into the blood to dilute sodium back to normal.

    Hyponatremia (< 135)

    Why: Water excess dilutes sodium (SIADH, excessive hypotonic fluids, heart failure).
    Danger: Water rushes into brain cells, causing swelling (cerebral edema), seizures, coma.

    Hypernatremia (> 145)

    Why: Pure water loss without replacement (fever, sweating, lack of water access in elderly).
    Danger: Brain cells shrink, pulling away from the skull and risking vascular tears.

    Golden Exam Rule: Never correct chronic hyponatremia faster than 0.5 mmol/L/hour (max 8–10 mmol/L in 24h)! Rapid elevation causes irreversible Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (paralysis).
    K⁺

    Potassium

    3.5 – 5.1 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Electrical Battery of Every Muscle & Heart Cell: 98% of potassium lives inside cells. It sets the baseline resting electrical voltage (-90 mV) of cardiac and skeletal muscles. Even tiny changes in blood potassium distort the heart's rhythm!

    The 0.4 pH Shift Rule

    For every 0.1 unit rise in arterial pH (alkalosis), blood potassium drops by 0.4 mmol/L as K⁺ shifts into cells. In acidosis (pH drops), K⁺ shifts out of cells into the bloodstream.

    Hypokalemia (< 3.5)

    Why: Diuretics (Loops/Thiazides), vomiting, diarrhea, insulin.
    ECG & Signs: Muscle cramps, weakness, flattened T-waves, prominent U-waves, long QU, Torsades de Pointes.

    Hyperkalemia (> 5.1)

    Why: Kidney failure, ACE inhibitors, tissue crush/burns, acidosis.
    ECG & Signs: Tall peaked "tented" T-waves, wide QRS, and lethal cardiac arrest.

    Golden Exam Rule: Never give potassium citrate in vomiting/alkalosis (citrate turns into base; use KCl!). And always check Magnesium: low Mg leaves ROMK wide open, leaking potassium into urine!
    Ca²⁺

    Calcium

    2.15 – 2.55 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Electrical Blanket on Nerve Membranes & Bone Matrix: 99% is stored in bones and teeth. In the bloodstream, extracellular calcium coats nerve membranes. When calcium is low, nerves become hyperexcitable and twitch; when calcium is high, nerves become numb and sluggish.

    Albumin Correction Formula

    40–45% of calcium is bound to albumin. If albumin is low (e.g. malnutrition, liver disease), measured total calcium appears falsely low. Corrected Ca = Measured Ca + 0.02 · (40 - Albumin).

    Hypocalcemia (< 2.15)

    Why: Hypoparathyroidism, Vitamin D deficiency, acute pancreatitis, Loop diuretics.
    Signs: Tetany, facial twitching (Chvostek), hand spasm (Trousseau), long QT interval.

    Hypercalcemia (> 2.55)

    Why: 90% is Primary Hyperparathyroidism or Cancer metastases.
    Signs: "Stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones", thirst, constipation, short QT.

    Diuretic Calcium Rule: "LOOPS LOSE CALCIUM" (used to treat hypercalcemia); "THIAZIDES SAVE CALCIUM" (reduces urine calcium by 40%, used for kidney stones, contraindicated in hypercalcemia!).
    Mg²⁺

    Magnesium

    0.66 – 1.07 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Spark Plug & Potassium Gatekeeper: Essential cofactor in over 300 enzyme reactions involving ATP. In the kidney, magnesium acts as a physical plug inside the ROMK channel pore. If magnesium is missing, the plug falls out, and potassium pours into the urine uncontrollably!

    Why Low Magnesium Causes Hypocalcemia Too

    Parathyroid glands need magnesium to manufacture and release PTH. Severe hypomagnesemia blunts PTH release, causing simultaneous hypocalcemia that won't respond until magnesium is replaced!

    Hypomagnesemia (< 0.66)

    Why: Chronic PPI use (blocks gut uptake), alcohol use, diarrhea, Carboplatin (occurs in 29–43%!).
    Signs: Tremors, hyperreflexia, refractory hypokalemia, Torsades de Pointes.

    Hypermagnesemia (> 1.07)

    Why: Excessive magnesium infusion (pre-eclampsia treatment) in renal impairment.
    Signs: Loss of deep tendon reflexes (early warning sign), bradycardia, respiratory arrest.

    Refractory Hypokalemia Trap: If giving potassium doesn't raise blood potassium, stop and check magnesium! You cannot fix hypokalemia until magnesium is restored to plug the ROMK channel.
    PO₄³⁻

    Phosphate

    0.81 – 1.45 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Cellular Battery (ATP) & Oxygen Delivery Key: Phosphate is the literal "P" in ATP (cellular energy currency). It also forms 2,3-DPG inside red blood cells, which commands hemoglobin to release oxygen to tissues. When phosphate crashes, cells run completely out of fuel!

    Refeeding Syndrome Mechanism

    When a malnourished or starving patient is suddenly fed carbohydrates, a massive insulin surge drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into cells to metabolize glucose. Blood phosphate crashes (< 0.3 mmol/L), paralyzing the diaphragm!

    Hypophosphatemia (< 0.81)

    Why: Refeeding syndrome, severe DKA treatment, chronic alcohol misuse.
    Signs: Diaphragmatic weakness (cannot wean off ventilator), rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis.

    Hyperphosphatemia (> 1.45)

    Why: Chronic kidney disease (kidneys cannot excrete PO₄³⁻), tumor lysis.
    Signs: Precipitates with calcium, causing metastatic calcification in arteries and severe skin itching.

    Refeeding Exam Pearl: In anorexia, starvation, or prolonged fasting: "Start low, go slow" with calories, and proactively replace phosphate, potassium, and magnesium before escalating nutrition.
    Cl⁻

    Chloride

    98 – 107 mmol/L
    Everyday Analogy & Core Role

    The Electrical Seesaw Partner to Bicarbonate: Chloride is the most abundant negative ion (anion) in extracellular fluid. Because the body must maintain electrical neutrality, Chloride and Bicarbonate act as partners on a seesaw: when Bicarbonate goes up (vomiting alkalosis), Chloride must go down!

    Saline-Responsive Metabolic Alkalosis

    Vomiting expels gastric HCl. To maintain volume and charge, the kidneys are trapped and cannot excrete excess bicarbonate into urine without chloride. Giving 0.9% NaCl provides chloride, curing the alkalosis.

    Hypochloremia (< 98)

    Why: Vomiting, NG tube suction, Loop or Thiazide diuretics.
    Sign: Urine Cl⁻ < 20 mmol/L confirms saline-responsive metabolic alkalosis.

    Hyperchloremia (> 107)

    Why: Infusing liters of 0.9% "Normal" Saline (contains 154 mmol/L Cl⁻ vs normal blood 102 mmol/L!).
    Sign: Crowds out bicarbonate, causing Hyperchloremic NAGMA (HARDUPS).

    The 0.9% Saline Irony: "Normal" Saline is not normal! Its chloride concentration is 154 mmol/L (50% higher than blood). Large infusions produce iatrogenic hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.

    PART I: Pharmacology, Diuretics & CKD Therapeutics
    DIURETICS · IONS
    “Thiazide diuretics can induce hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia and (low grade) hypercalcemia”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    HypoK & HypoMg: Thiazides block the NCC cotransporter in the DCT. This dumps massive Na⁺ and fluid into the Collecting Duct. There, ENaC rapidly reabsorbs Na⁺, generating an intense lumen-negative electrical gradient (-15 to -30 mV) that pulls positively charged K⁺ (ROMK) and Mg²⁺ out into the urine.
    Hypercalcemia: Intracellular Na⁺ drops in DCT cells, speeding up the basolateral Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX1). This pulls Ca²⁺ into the blood via apical TRPV5 channels.

    Clinical Vignette & Key Clue

    Loops LOSE Calcium; Thiazides SAVE Calcium. If a patient on a diuretic has elevated serum calcium (> 2.6 mmol/L), suspect thiazide therapy or unmasked primary hyperparathyroidism.

    Target: NCC in DCT
    DIURETICS · WATER
    “Thiazide diuretics can induce severe hyponatremia (impairment of urinary dilution due to reduced sodium reabsorption in distal tubule)”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    The Distal Convoluted Tubule is the kidney's "Cortical Diluting Segment": it is completely impermeable to water. Normally, removing salt without water dilutes urine down to 50 mOsm/kg. Thiazides block NCC, directly preventing urine dilution! Simultaneously, mild volume depletion triggers pituitary ADH release, reabsorbing pure water in the collecting duct.

    Clinical Vignette & Key Clue

    Why not Loop diuretics? Loops destroy the medullary gradient, so ADH cannot concentrate urine. Thiazides spare the medulla: ADH works at 100% while DCT dilution is disabled, producing catastrophic hyponatremia (e.g. Na⁺ = 114 mmol/L in elderly women).

    Mechanism: Loss of dilution
    DIURETICS · METABOLIC
    “Thiazide diuretics can induce hyperuricemia and gout”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Two distinct mechanisms raise uric acid: 1) Increased proximal reabsorption: Mild volume depletion stimulates proximal tubule fluid reabsorption via URAT1, dragging uric acid into blood. 2) Secretory competition: Thiazide molecules compete with uric acid for basolateral Organic Anion Transporters (OAT1/OAT3), directly impairing uric acid secretion.

    Clinical Vignette & Key Clue

    A hypertensive patient develops an acute hot, red, swollen first toe (podagra) 3 weeks after starting HCTZ. Management: Treat acute attack (colchicine/NSAIDs), stop thiazide, and switch to Losartan (an ARB that uniquely inhibits URAT1 to lower uric acid!).

    Clue: URAT1 & OAT Switch to Losartan
    PHARMACOLOGY · VASCULAR
    “Calcium antagonists can induce leg edema (increased capillary hydrostatic pressure from greater vasodilation of pre-capillary than post-capillary vessels)”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Dihydropyridines (Amlodipine, Nifedipine) selectively dilate pre-capillary arterioles while leaving post-capillary venules unchanged. High arterial pressure transmits straight into fragile capillaries (elevated Pc ↑), forcing plasma transudate into interstitial tissue.

    Clinical Trap: Avoid Diuretics

    This is NOT fluid overload! Total body sodium is normal. Giving Furosemide causes pre-renal azotemia, hypotension, and reflex tachycardia. Correct Action: Lower CCB dose or add an ACEi/ARB (which dilates post-capillary venules, restoring balanced microvascular pressure).

    Mechanism: Pc Transudation Fix: Add ACEi / ARB
    RAAS · POTASSIUM
    “ACE inhibitors, sartans, renin inhibitors and aldosterone inhibitors can induce hyperkalemia”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Aldosterone is the kidney's master switch for potassium excretion. By activating ENaC and basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPases in principal cells, it creates the negative charge that propels K⁺ through ROMK channels. Blocking the RAAS anywhere (renin → ACE → AT₁ receptor → MR) eliminates aldosterone signaling, shutting down K⁺ excretion.

    Clinical Vignette & Key Clue

    Greatest danger in CKD, diabetes (hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism / Type IV RTA), and concurrent NSAID use. Always measure serum K⁺ and creatinine within 1–2 weeks of starting or increasing RAAS blockers.

    Target: Principal cell ENaC/ROMK
    DRUGS · MULTI-ION
    “IPP can induce severe hypomagnesemia (with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia)”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Chronic Proton Pump Inhibitor therapy (> 3–12 months) raises colonic luminal pH, disabling the pH-sensitive TRPM6 magnesium channel in the bowel.
    • Why Hypokalemia? Intracellular Mg²⁺ is the physical "cork" blocking ROMK. Without Mg, ROMK stays wide open, dumping potassium.
    • Why Hypocalcemia? Low Mg cripples adenylate cyclase in parathyroid cells, causing PTH gland failure & end-organ PTH resistance.

    Clinical Vignette & Key Clue

    Patient on chronic Pantoprazole presents with tetany (Chvostek/Trousseau sign), low calcium, low potassium, and low/normal PTH. You cannot correct K⁺ until you replace Mg²⁺! Stop the PPI and give IV magnesium sulfate.

    Triad: Low Mg + Low K + Low Ca
    CKD · ACID-BASE
    “CKD induced acidosis has to be treated with bicarbonate (to improve renal prognosis, decrease protein catabolism, and prevent bone loss)”
    Professor's 3 Pillars for Bicarbonate

    1) Improve Renal Prognosis: Surviving nephrons produce excess endothelin & complement to excrete acid, driving interstitial fibrosis and faster GFR loss.
    2) Decrease Protein Catabolism: Acidemia activates the ATP-dependent ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, causing uremic muscle wasting.
    3) Prevent Bone Loss: Acid is buffered by bone carbonate/phosphate, accelerating renal osteodystrophy.

    Clinical Action & Target

    Prescribe oral Sodium Bicarbonate (NaHCO₃, 500 mg–1 g TID) or Sodium Citrate to maintain serum bicarbonate strictly between 22 and 26 mmol/L.

    Target: HCO₃⁻ ≥ 22 mmol/L Oral NaHCO₃
    CKD · HEMATOLOGY
    “CKD associated anemia (if TSAT ≤ 30% and Ferritin ≤ 500 ng/mL) has to be treated with intravenous iron before giving erythropoietin”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Uremia causes systemic inflammation, elevating Hepcidin. Hepcidin destroys Ferroportin, locking iron inside macrophages (Functional Iron Deficiency). Giving Erythropoietin (EPO) without adequate bioavailable iron causes bone marrow resistance and fails to raise hemoglobin!

    Exam Numbers to Memorize

    Give IV iron if TSAT ≤ 30% and Ferritin ≤ 500 ng/mL. Only once iron stores are loaded, initiate ESA (Darbepoetin/EPO) targeting Hb 10–11.5 g/dL (never $>13$ due to stroke risk).

    Cutoffs: TSAT ≤ 30%, Ferritin ≤ 500 IV Iron FIRST
    PART II: Diagnostic Syndromes, Renovascular & Endocrine Pearls
    ENDOCRINE · HTN
    “Hypertension + hypokalemia → suspect a mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) excess”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome / Adrenal Hyperplasia): Autonomous aldosterone hypersecretion forces principal cells to reabsorb excess Na⁺ through ENaC (causing hypertension) while dumping K⁺ via ROMK (hypokalemia) and H⁺ via α-intercalated cells (metabolic alkalosis).

    Diagnostic Workup

    Measure the Aldosterone-to-Renin Ratio (ARR): high aldosterone + suppressed renin (PRA < 1.0) yields a ratio $>20–30$. Confirm with Saline Infusion or Oral Salt Loading test.

    Clue: High ARR (Aldo ↑ / Renin ↓) Conn Syndrome
    ENDOCRINE · CUSHING
    “Hypertension + hypokalemia + hyperglycemia → suspect a glucocorticoid (cortisol) excess”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Cushing Syndrome: Cortisol causes gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance (hyperglycemia). In massive excess (or ectopic ACTH), cortisol saturates the protective renal enzyme 11β-HSD2, allowing cortisol to bind directly to Mineralocorticoid Receptors with equal potency to aldosterone (hypertension + severe hypokalemia!).

    Diagnostic Triad to Recognize

    HTN + Low K⁺ + High Glucose = Cushing! Confirm with 24h urinary free cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol, or 1 mg overnight Dexamethasone test.

    Mechanism: 11β-HSD2 saturation Cushing Syndrome
    RENOVASCULAR · IMAGING
    “Resistant hypertension in a young subject (persistent hypertension despite 3 medications including a diuretic); perform a duplex ultrasound of kidney arteries to rule out stenosis”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Fibromuscular Dysplasia (FMD): In young patients (especially females $<35$), medial fibroplasia creates "string-of-beads" stenoses. Renal underperfusion triggers relentless renin secretion (Goldblatt kidney), causing severe renovascular hypertension refractory to triple-therapy.

    Diagnostic Criteria & Imaging

    Resistant HTN = BP above goal despite ≥ 3 drugs at optimal doses (one MUST be a diuretic). First imaging test: Renal Artery Duplex Ultrasound.

    Young = FMD; Elderly = Atherosclerosis Duplex Ultrasound
    GLOMERULUS · NEPHRITIC
    “Reduced GFR + proteinuria + microhematuria + hypertension → suspect a glomerular disease”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Nephritic Syndrome: Glomerular capillary inflammation causes basement membrane breaks. Red blood cells squeeze through and get deformed into dysmorphic acanthocytes and diagnostic RBC casts. Reduced glomerular surface area lowers GFR, while impaired sodium excretion produces hypertension and edema.

    Clinical Action & Diagnosis

    Perform fresh urine sediment microscopy (look for dysmorphic RBCs > 40% and red cell casts) and urgently arrange a percutaneous renal biopsy.

    Hallmark: Dysmorphic RBCs & RBC Casts Renal Biopsy
    GLOMERULUS · IGA
    “IgA nephropathy is the most frequent glomerulonephritis in adults”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Berger Disease: Abnormal galactose-deficient IgA1 forms circulating immune complexes that deposit directly in the glomerular mesangium, triggering mesangioproliferative inflammation and glomerulosclerosis.

    Key Differential: IgA vs. PSGN

    IgA vs PSGN Timing: IgA causes macroscopic tea-colored hematuria 1–2 days after a cold (synpharyngitic) with normal C3. PSGN occurs 1–3 weeks after infection and has low C3!

    Onset: 1–2 days after URI (Normal C3) Mesangial IgA
    CKD · NEPHROPROTECTION
    “To prevent progression of CKD, prescribe ACE inhibitors or sartans, and/or glucose tubular reuptake (SGLT2) inhibitors”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Intraglomerular hypertension destroys remaining nephrons. • ACEi / ARBs: Dilate the efferent arteriole (outflow), relieving intraglomerular pressure.
    • SGLT2i (Dapagliflozin): Delivers Na⁺ to macula densa, restoring tubuloglomerular feedback to constrict the afferent arteriole (inflow). Together they halt proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis!

    Clinical Clue: Expected eGFR Dip

    Starting either drug causes an expected, hemodynamically benign eGFR drop (up to 30%). Do NOT discontinue the drug; it confirms successful pressure relief!

    Efferent Dilation + Afferent Constriction
    POTASSIUM · EMERGENCY
    “Correct hypokalemia before giving insulin”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Insulin is the most potent activator of cellular Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, pumping extracellular K⁺ into cells within minutes. In DKA or HHS, total-body potassium is depleted. If serum K⁺ < 3.3 mmol/L, infusing insulin drives potassium into cells, precipitating sudden fatal ventricular arrhythmias or respiratory arrest!

    Emergency Protocol

    If K⁺ < 3.3 mmol/L, HOLD INSULIN. Give IV KCl (20–30 mEq/h) until K⁺ ≥ 3.3. Only then start insulin with continuous potassium co-infusion.

    Cutoff: Never give insulin if K⁺ < 3.3
    POTASSIUM · SHIFTS
    “Consider potassium shift when deciding about potassium substitution (cave: acidosis can mask hypokalemia)”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Transcellular Shift Rule: In metabolic acidosis, excess H⁺ enters cells to be buffered by intracellular proteins; to maintain electrical neutrality, intracellular K⁺ is displaced into the bloodstream.
    Quantitative Rule: Every 0.1 drop in pH raises blood K⁺ by ~0.4 mmol/L.

    Clinical Trap: Masked Hypokalemia

    A patient with severe diarrhea presents with pH = 7.10 and K⁺ = 4.0 mmol/L ("normal"). Corrected for normal pH (7.40), true baseline K⁺ is only 2.8 mmol/L! Correcting acidosis without potassium will plunge them into severe hypokalemia.

    Formula: ΔK = 0.4 × (7.40 - pH) / 0.1
    SODIUM · TONICITY
    “Actively correct hyponatremia only if it is hypoosmolar”
    Pathophysiology & Mechanism

    Hyponatremia is only dangerous if it causes brain cell swelling (cerebral edema). Water moves exclusively along effective osmotic gradients.
    • Hypertonic (> 295 mOsm/kg): Severe hyperglycemia (DKA) pulls water OUT of brain cells (cellular dehydration!). Treat with insulin, never saline.
    • Isotonic (280–295 mOsm/kg): Pseudohyponatremia from lipids/proteins; brain cells are completely unaffected.

    Diagnostic Rule: Check Osmolality First

    Measure Measured Serum Osmolality First! Only Hypotonic Hyponatremia (< 280 mOsm/kg) causes cerebral edema and requires active sodium correction.

    Rule: Step 1 = Serum Osmolality
    SODIUM · SIADH
    “Hyponatremia + serum hypoosmolality + urinary hyperosmolality + urinary sodium > 20 (40) (+ low uric acid) → suspect SIADH”
    Decoding the 4 SIADH Criteria

    1) Serum Hypoosmolality: Excess ADH reabsorbs pure water, diluting blood (Sosm < 275).
    2) Urinary Hyperosmolality (Uosm > 100): ADH opens collecting duct aquaporins, improperly concentrating urine during hypoosmolality.
    3) Urinary Na⁺ > 20–40 mmol/L (Euvolemia): Water retention slightly expands volume, triggering ANP/BNP and shutting down proximal Na⁺ reabsorption.
    4) Low Uric Acid (< 240 µmol/L): Volume expansion downregulates URAT1, dumping uric acid into urine (high specificity for SIADH!).

    Treatment Protocol

    First-line therapy: Fluid restriction (< 800–1000 mL/day). If severe neurological symptoms (seizures, coma): infuse hypertonic 3% NaCl (100 mL bolus over 10 min) to raise Na⁺ by 4–6 mmol/L.

    Tetrad: Low S-Osm + High U-Osm + High U-Na + Low Uric
