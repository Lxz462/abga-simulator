    The 6-Step ABG Interpretation Guide

    A simple, student-friendly roadmap to break down any arterial blood gas without confusion.

    The Big Picture: Two Organs on a Seesaw
    1. Lungs control CO₂ (Acid)

    Carbon dioxide dissolves into carbonic acid. You breathe it out. Fast reaction: responds in minutes by breathing faster or slower.

    2. Kidneys control HCO₃⁻ (Base)

    Bicarbonate is your liquid buffer sponge. Kidneys save or pee it out. Slower reaction: takes 2 to 3 days to fully adapt.

    Step 1 Check the pH — Is the blood Acidic or Basic?

    Normal healthy arterial blood pH is tightly defended between 7.35 and 7.45.

    pH < 7.35 = Acidemia

    Too much acid or too little base in the blood.

    7.35 – 7.45 = Normal

    Normal balance (or two severe disorders cancelling out!).

    pH > 7.45 = Alkalemia

    Too much base or too little acid in the blood.

    Step 2 Find the Culprit — Who caused the problem?

    Look at both numbers: PaCO₂ (Lungs = acid gas) and HCO₃⁻ (Kidneys = base sponge). Ask yourself: Which one explains why the pH moved?

    If pH is Low (Acidic < 7.35):
    • If PaCO₂ is HIGH (> 6.0 kPa / 45 mmHg): Lungs trapped acid gas → Respiratory Acidosis (e.g. stopped breathing).
    • If HCO₃⁻ is LOW (< 22 mmol/L): Body lost base or gathered acid → Metabolic Acidosis (e.g. DKA, diarrhea).
    If pH is High (Basic > 7.45):
    • If PaCO₂ is LOW (< 4.7 kPa / 35 mmHg): Lungs blew off too much acid → Respiratory Alkalosis (e.g. hyperventilating).
    • If HCO₃⁻ is HIGH (> 26 mmol/L): Body accumulated base or lost stomach acid → Metabolic Alkalosis (e.g. vomiting).
    Step 3 Check Compensation — Is the other organ trying to help?

    When one organ fails, the healthy organ works overtime to drag the pH back toward safe levels.

    The Golden Law: The body never overcompensates. It never turns an acidosis into an alkalosis.
    Winter's Rule of Thumb (for Metabolic Acidosis): If bicarbonate drops, the lungs MUST breathe faster to lower PaCO₂. Expected PaCO₂ (mmHg) ≈ 1.5 × [HCO₃⁻] + 8 (in kPa: [HCO₃⁻] × 0.2 + 1.1). If measured PaCO₂ is higher than expected, the patient is also in respiratory failure!
    Step 4 If Metabolic Acidosis: Calculate the Anion Gap ("The Mystery Acid Test")

    Blood is always electrically neutral. Normally, Sodium (positive) is balanced by Chloride and Bicarbonate (negative), leaving a normal gap of 12 ± 2 mmol/L (10 to 14 mmol/L) for unmeasured proteins (mainly albumin).

    Anion Gap = Na⁺ - (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)   [Normal average: 12 mmol/L (range: 10 – 14 mmol/L)]
    High Anion Gap (> 14) — "Mystery Acid"

    Dangerous unmeasured acids (like ketones, lactic acid, or poisons) entered the blood and destroyed bicarbonate. Look at the GOLD MARK tab!

    Normal Anion Gap (10–14) — "Base Leak"

    No mystery acid. The body simply lost bicarbonate directly (like severe diarrhea), and the kidneys held onto extra Chloride to compensate. Look at HARDUPS!

    Step 5 Check the Delta Ratio — Is a second disorder hiding in secret?

    When the Anion Gap is high, compare how much the gap opened up (ΔAG = AG - 12) versus how much bicarbonate was lost (ΔHCO₃ = 24 - HCO₃⁻).

    Ratio ~ 1.0 (0.8–1.6)

    Pure High Anion Gap Acidosis. Every 1 molecule of mystery acid consumed exactly 1 molecule of bicarbonate (e.g. pure DKA).

    Ratio > 2.0

    Hidden Metabolic Alkalosis! Bicarbonate is higher than expected—for example, a diabetic patient who has also been violently vomiting stomach acid.

    Ratio < 0.8

    Mixed Acidosis! Patient has both high-gap and normal-gap acidosis (e.g. DKA plus severe diarrhea).

    Step 6 Check Oxygenation & the A-a Gradient

    Normal arterial oxygen (PaO₂) is 10.0 to 13.3 kPa (75 to 100 mmHg) on room air. If oxygen is low, the Alveolar-Arterial (A-a) Gradient answers: Are the lungs broken, or is the patient just not breathing?

    Normal A-a Gradient (Lungs are healthy):

    Oxygen easily crosses the lung membranes, but not enough air is reaching the alveoli (e.g., opioid overdose or chest muscle weakness). Reverse the hypoventilation and oxygen returns to normal!

    High A-a Gradient (Lungs are diseased):

    Oxygen cannot cross into the blood due to physical lung damage, fluid, or blood clots (e.g., pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary edema, ARDS).

    Exam Acronym Compendium: GOLD MARK & HARDUPS

    The modern, high-yield memory aids for diagnosing metabolic acidoses on medical licensing exams.

    Why does the Anion Gap open up? (The "Sponge" Analogy)

    Think of circulating bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) as a protective sponge that absorbs acid. In health, positive sodium ions (Na⁺) are balanced by negative chloride (Cl⁻) and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), with a small normal gap of 10–14 mmol/L. When abnormal organic acids enter your blood, they soak into the sponge, destroying bicarbonate. But the acid's negative partner (formate, lactate, ketones) stays behind unmeasured, blowing open a High Anion Gap.

    GOLD MARK — High Anion Gap Acidosis (HAGMA) Replaces older MUDPILES
    G — Glycols (Antifreeze & Brake Fluid) Toxin

    Drinking ethylene glycol (car antifreeze) or propylene glycol. The liver turns it into oxalic acid, which binds calcium and creates needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystals that plug and destroy the kidneys.

    Exam Clue: Acute renal failure + crystals in urine + urine glows under Wood's UV lamp. Antidote: Fomepizole.
    O — Oxoproline (Tylenol in Malnutrition) Drug Reaction

    Occurs in malnourished, elderly, or septic patients taking regular paracetamol (acetaminophen). Depletion of liver glutathione unlocks an enzyme pathway that churns out 5-oxoproline acid.

    Exam Clue: Unexplained high anion gap in a paracetamol user with completely NORMAL lactate and glucose!
    L — L-Lactate (Shock & Oxygen Starvation) Commonest Inpatient

    The most frequent acidosis in the ICU. When tissues don't get enough blood flow or oxygen (septic shock, cardiac arrest, ischemic bowel), cells switch to emergency anaerobic metabolism, producing massive L-lactic acid.

    Exam Clue: Low blood pressure, cold extremities, serum lactate > 2.0–4.0 mmol/L. Also seen in Metformin toxicity.
    D — D-Lactate (Short Bowel Bacteria) Gut Fermentation

    A rare exam favorite! In patients who had surgical bowel resections (short bowel syndrome), undigested carbs reach colonic bacteria, which ferment them into D-lactate (a mirror molecule human cells cannot process).

    Exam Clue: Confusion and slurred speech ("acting drunk") after pasta/bread, but standard hospital lactate test is NORMAL!
    M — Methanol (Moonshine & Windshield Fluid) Toxin

    Ingestion of adulterated homemade spirits or windshield washer solvent. The liver converts methanol into formic acid, which poisons mitochondrial respiration and destroys the optic nerve.

    Exam Clue: "Snowstorm" visual loss, dilated pupils, massive osmolal gap. Antidote: Fomepizole or IV ethanol.
    A — Aspirin / Salicylates (Classic Mixed Disturbance) Overdose

    Aspirin overdose causes a unique 2-stage disorder: directly stimulates the brain breathing center (causing early respiratory alkalosis), then poisons cellular power plants (causing high-gap metabolic acidosis).

    Exam Clue: Triad of Tinnitus (ringing ears) + High Fever + Rapid breathing, with near-normal pH masking dual disaster!
    R — Renal Failure (Uremia) Organ Failure

    Every day, body metabolism generates waste acids (sulfates, phosphates, urate). When the kidneys stop filtering urine, these natural daily metabolic acids pile up in the blood.

    Exam Clue: High BUN / Urea, high creatinine, oliguria (no urine output), and high potassium.
    K — Ketoacidosis (Diabetes, Alcohol, Starvation) Metabolic

    When cells cannot access glucose for fuel, the liver burns fat into acidic ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate).

    Exam Clue: DKA = High glucose + fruity breath + Kussmaul deep breathing. AKA = Binge drinker vomiting without food with normal/low glucose.
    HARDUPS — Normal Anion Gap (Hyperchloremic) Acidosis (NAGMA) Anion Gap is 10–14

    Why is the gap normal? Because there is NO mystery acid! The body simply lost bicarbonate base directly (usually through diarrhea or urine). To keep electrical neutrality, the kidneys grab extra Chloride (Cl⁻) from the blood. Chloride rises by the exact amount Bicarbonate fell, keeping the gap perfectly normal!

    H
    Hyperalimentation: IV parenteral nutrition (TPN) with excess chloride salts.
    A
    Acetazolamide: Glaucoma/altitude drug that stops kidneys from saving bicarbonate.
    R
    Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA): Kidneys can't excrete acid or reclaim bicarbonate.
    D
    Diarrhea (#1 Cause!): Lower bowel fluid is rich in base. Diarrhea poops out your bicarbonate.
    U
    Ureteral Diversion: Surgical routing of urine into bowel (colon absorbs Cl⁻, dumps HCO₃⁻).
    P
    Pancreatic Fistula: Pancreatic juice is rich in bicarbonate; surgical leaks cause severe base deficit.
    S
    Saline Overload (0.9% NaCl): Infusing liters of "normal" saline infuses 154 mmol/L of chloride (normal blood is only ~102), crowding out bicarbonate.
