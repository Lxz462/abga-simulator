    The 6-Step ABG Interpretation Guide

    A clear, organized clinical roadmap to break down any arterial blood gas without confusion.

    1. pH → 2. Culprit → 3. Compensation → 4. Anion Gap → 5. Delta Ratio → 6. Oxygenation
    The Big Picture: Two Organs on a Seesaw
    1. Lungs control CO₂ (Acid)

    Carbon dioxide dissolves into carbonic acid. You breathe it out. Fast reaction: responds in minutes by breathing faster or slower.

    2. Kidneys control HCO₃⁻ (Base)

    Bicarbonate is your liquid buffer sponge. Kidneys save or pee it out. Slower reaction: takes 2 to 3 days to fully adapt.

    Step 1

    Check the pH — Is the blood Acidic, Normal, or Basic?

    Normal healthy arterial blood pH is tightly defended between 7.35 and 7.45.

    pH < 7.35 = Acidemia

    Too much acid or too little base in the blood.

    7.35 – 7.45 = Normal

    Normal balance (or two severe disorders cancelling out!).

    pH > 7.45 = Alkalemia

    Too much base or too little acid in the blood.

    Step 2

    Find the Culprit — PaCO₂ (Lungs) vs HCO₃⁻ (Kidneys)

    Look at both numbers: PaCO₂ (Lungs = acid gas) and HCO₃⁻ (Kidneys = base sponge). Ask yourself: Which one explains why the pH moved?

    If pH is Low (Acidic < 7.35):
    • PaCO₂ HIGH (> 6.0 kPa / 45 mmHg): Lungs trapped acid gas → Respiratory Acidosis
    • HCO₃⁻ LOW (< 22 mmol/L): Body lost base or gathered acid → Metabolic Acidosis
    If pH is High (Basic > 7.45):
    • PaCO₂ LOW (< 4.7 kPa / 35 mmHg): Lungs blew off too much acid → Respiratory Alkalosis
    • HCO₃⁻ HIGH (> 26 mmol/L): Body accumulated base or lost acid → Metabolic Alkalosis
    Step 3

    Check Compensation — Is the other organ trying to help?

    When one organ fails, the healthy organ works overtime to drag the pH back toward safe levels.

    The Golden Law: The body never overcompensates. It never turns an acidosis into an alkalosis.
    Winter's Rule (for Metabolic Acidosis): If bicarbonate drops, the lungs MUST breathe faster to lower PaCO₂.
    Expected PaCO₂ (mmHg) ≈ 1.5 × [HCO₃⁻] + 8 ± 2  |  in kPa: [HCO₃⁻] × 0.2 + 1.1

    If measured PaCO₂ is higher than expected, the patient is also in respiratory failure!

    Step 4

    If Metabolic Acidosis: Calculate the Anion Gap

    Blood is always electrically neutral. Normally, Sodium (positive) is balanced by Chloride and Bicarbonate (negative), leaving a normal gap of 10 to 14 mmol/L (average 12) for unmeasured proteins (chiefly albumin).

    Anion Gap = Na⁺ - (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)   [Normal: 10 – 14 mmol/L]
    High Anion Gap (> 14) — "Mystery Acid"

    Dangerous unmeasured acids (like ketones, lactic acid, or poisons) entered the blood and destroyed bicarbonate. Look at the GOLD MARK tab!

    Normal Anion Gap (10–14) — "Base Leak"

    No mystery acid. The body simply lost bicarbonate directly (like severe diarrhea), and kidneys conserved Chloride to compensate. Look at HARDUPS!

    Step 5

    Check the Delta Ratio (ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻) — Hidden Mixed Disorders

    When Anion Gap is elevated, compare how much the gap opened up (ΔAG = AG - 12) versus how much bicarbonate was consumed (ΔHCO₃ = 24 - HCO₃⁻).

    Ratio ~ 1.0 (0.8–1.6)

    Pure HAGMA: Exactly 1 molecule of unmeasured acid consumed 1 molecule of bicarbonate (e.g. uncomplicated DKA).

    Ratio > 2.0

    Hidden Metabolic Alkalosis! Bicarbonate is higher than expected—for example, a diabetic patient who has also been vomiting stomach acid.

    Ratio < 0.8

    Mixed Acidosis! Patient has both high-gap and normal-gap acidosis (e.g. DKA plus severe diarrhea).

    Step 6

    Check Oxygenation & the A-a Gradient

    Normal arterial oxygen (PaO₂) is 10.0 to 13.3 kPa (75 to 100 mmHg) on room air. The A-a Gradient answers: Are the lungs broken, or is the patient just not breathing?

    Normal A-a Gradient (Lungs Healthy):

    Oxygen easily crosses the lung membranes, but not enough air is reaching the alveoli (e.g. opioid overdose, chest wall muscle weakness). Reverse the hypoventilation and oxygen normalizes!

    High A-a Gradient (Lungs Diseased):

    Oxygen cannot cross into the blood due to physical lung damage, fluid, or blood clots (e.g. pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary edema, ARDS).

    Exam Acronym Compendium: GOLD MARK & HARDUPS

    The modern, high-yield memory aids for diagnosing metabolic acidoses on medical licensing exams.

    Why does the Anion Gap open up? (The "Sponge" Analogy)

    Think of circulating bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) as a protective sponge that absorbs acid. In health, positive sodium ions (Na⁺) are balanced by negative chloride (Cl⁻) and bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), with a small normal gap of 10–14 mmol/L. When abnormal organic acids enter your blood, they soak into the sponge, destroying bicarbonate. But the acid's unmeasured negative partner (formate, lactate, ketones) stays behind, blowing open a High Anion Gap.

    GOLD MARK — High Anion Gap Acidosis (HAGMA)
    Modern update to MUDPILES
    G

    Glycols (Antifreeze & Brake Fluid)

    Toxin

    Ingestion of ethylene glycol or propylene glycol. The liver turns it into oxalic acid, forming needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystals that plug and destroy kidneys.

    Exam Clue: Acute renal failure + envelope crystals in urine + urine glows under Wood's UV lamp. Antidote: Fomepizole.
    O

    Oxoproline (Tylenol in Malnutrition)

    Drug Reaction

    Occurs in malnourished, elderly, or septic patients taking regular paracetamol (acetaminophen). Depletion of glutathione unlocks an enzyme pathway churning out 5-oxoproline acid.

    Exam Clue: Unexplained high anion gap in a paracetamol user with completely NORMAL lactate and glucose!
    L

    L-Lactate (Shock & Hypoxia)

    Commonest ICU

    The most frequent acidosis in hospital. Tissues deprived of oxygen (sepsis, cardiac arrest, mesenteric ischemia) switch to anaerobic glycolysis, generating massive L-lactic acid.

    Exam Clue: Hypotension, mottling, serum lactate > 2.0–4.0 mmol/L. Also seen in Metformin accumulation.
    D

    D-Lactate (Short Bowel Syndrome)

    Gut Bacteria

    In short bowel surgical resections, undigested carbs reach colonic flora, which ferment them into D-lactate (a mirror enantiomer human cells cannot metabolize).

    Exam Clue: Neurological ataxia / slurred speech ("acting drunk") after carbs, while standard hospital lactate assay is NORMAL!
    M

    Methanol (Moonshine / Solvent)

    Toxin

    Ingestion of moonshine or windshield fluid. Liver alcohol dehydrogenase converts methanol into cytotoxic formic acid, which destroys optic nerves and basal ganglia.

    Exam Clue: "Snowstorm" blindness, fixed dilated pupils, massive osmolal gap. Antidote: Fomepizole or IV ethanol.
    A

    Aspirin / Salicylates (Classic Mixed)

    Overdose

    Aspirin uncouples oxidative phosphorylation and hyper-stimulates brainstem respiratory center, causing early respiratory alkalosis followed by high-gap metabolic acidosis.

    Exam Clue: Triad of Tinnitus + Hyperthermia + Tachypnea with deceptively near-normal pH.
    R

    Renal Failure (Uremia)

    Organ Failure

    Body metabolism generates waste acids daily (sulfates, phosphates, urate). When glomerular filtration drops severely (GFR < 15–20), these unmeasured daily acids accumulate in blood.

    Exam Clue: Markedly elevated BUN/Creatinine, oliguria, hyperkalemia, and uremic symptoms.
    K

    Ketoacidosis (DKA, AKA, Starvation)

    Metabolic

    When cells lack intracellular glucose, the liver rapidly oxidizes fatty acids into ketone anions (beta-hydroxybutyrate & acetoacetate).

    Exam Clue: DKA: Hyperglycemia + Kussmaul breathing. AKA: Binge drinker vomiting without food with normal/low glucose.
    HARDUPS — Normal Anion Gap (Hyperchloremic) Acidosis (NAGMA)
    Anion Gap: 10–14

    Why is the gap normal? Because there is NO mystery acid! The body lost bicarbonate base directly (diarrhea or renal leakage). To maintain electrical charge, kidneys conserve Chloride (Cl⁻). Chloride rises by the exact amount Bicarbonate fell, keeping the gap completely normal!

    H
    Hyperalimentation: IV parenteral nutrition (TPN) with excess chloride salts.
    A
    Acetazolamide: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that stops proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation.
    R
    Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA): Proximal or distal nephron tubules fail to excrete H⁺ or save HCO₃⁻.
    D
    Diarrhea (#1 Cause!): Bowel secretions are loaded with bicarbonate. Diarrhea dumps base.
    U
    Ureteral Diversion: Surgical routing of urine into bowel (colon epithelium absorbs Cl⁻, secretes HCO₃⁻).
    P
    Pancreatic Fistula: Pancreatic juice is rich in bicarbonate; surgical fistulas cause profound base loss.
    S
    Saline 0.9% Excess: Liters of normal saline (Cl⁻ 154 mmol/L) crowd out bicarbonate, inducing iatrogenic hyperchloremic acidosis.
